Why it matters

  1. Ice decides where the first Moon base goes. It means water, oxygen and rocket fuel on site.
  2. Radar can't tell ice from rocks alone. High "twisted echo" (CPR) comes from both — scientists have debated it for over a decade.
  3. Earth analogs are still picked mostly by hand. GroundTwin searches with data and shows where each analogy breaks.

Radar Twin — is it ice or rock?

1

The radar sends a twisted (circularly polarized) wave.

2

Smooth ground flips the twist. Rocks and ice keep part of it.

3

CPR = kept ÷ flipped. High CPR means rocks or ice — so we need Earth's answer key.

    Mars Field Twin: Mangystau — the floor of a vanished sea

    Mars once had lakes in craters like Gale and Jezero; then the water left, and layered rocks, salts and dust remained. Mangystau in western Kazakhstan is the floor of the ancient Tethys Sea: striped sedimentary cliffs, salt flats (sors) and giant stone concretions, in a dry land with almost no plants.

    The Karagiye Depression, about 132 m below sea level, is the lowest point in Kazakhstan; it formed later, as rocks dissolved. Travel writing calls Mangystau a "Martian landscape", but we found no scientific study of it as a Mars analog — GroundTwin tests the idea with NASA data.

    Honest limits

    • Earth cannot reproduce low gravity, vacuum, the thin Martian air or space radiation.
    • RCM (C-band, ~5.5 cm) and Mini-RF (S-band, 12.6 cm) use different wavelengths: we compare the order of classes and the inside/outside contrast, not exact numbers.
    • Earth ice is cleaner than lunar ice mixed with regolith; moisture changes radar on Earth, so we use dry winter scenes.
    • The global grid is coarse: GroundTwin is a screening tool, not a replacement for field work.
    • Target profiles and weights are our choices — all of them are visible and editable.
